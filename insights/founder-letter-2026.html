2026 Founder Letter: Building for the Long Term

September 2026 · 8 min read

To our partners, employees, and everyone interested in what X Group is building:

When we formalized X Group as a Canadian operating holding company in 2026, we did not set out to look large. We set out to make what we already had — a collection of real operating companies with shared owners, systems and management — run better and last longer. This letter explains why we think that matters, what we have learned, and what we got wrong along the way.

The thesis

The strongest version of this group is not bigger than the sum of its parts. It is the opposite. Strong operating companies do not need a parent to pretend they are something they are not — they need a parent that handles the machinery that matters only at scale.

Our thesis is simple: centralize the functions that benefit from scale — finance, procurement, people, technology, marketing and strategy — while keeping operating companies focused on their customers and markets. That leaves the parent to do only what it should do: manage shared infrastructure, decide where capital goes next, and keep the group disciplined about what it will and will not do.

We have now run this model operationally for years. We own [TO BE PROVIDED: number] operating companies across [TO BE PROVIDED: number] towns and cities. We have fixed over [TO BE PROVIDED: number] garage doors in the Lower Mainland and beyond. We have been operating since 2007, which means we have been through booms, recessions, a global pandemic, supply-chain chaos and rate cycles. We have kept our people, kept our customers and kept our standards through all of it. The model works.

What we got right

We were right that operating discipline beats size. A small, lean operation with good systems will outrun a large, bloated one every time. We centralized procurement and negotiated contracts that give each operating company buying power they could never have alone — without them losing control of their customer relationships or their local decision-making. That is not cosmetic; it is tens of thousands of dollars per company per year in lower cost of goods sold.

We were right that the infrastructure machinery matters. When you have multiple operating companies running on separate systems, you do not have visibility into what actually works. When you have one platform underneath them — one accounting system, one people system, one technology stack — you can see patterns, share learning, and scale discipline. That visibility is the actual value of a parent.

We were right to stay selective. We have turned away opportunities that looked large on paper but did not fit our thesis. A business that does not need centralized infrastructure is not something we should acquire. A market where we do not have real operating expertise is not somewhere we should go. Discipline is harder than growth, but it is what makes the difference between a holding company and a private equity pile.

What we got wrong

We underestimated how long it takes to integrate systems. When we began combining operating companies onto shared infrastructure, we thought the process would be quick and clean. It was neither. Converting data from legacy systems, retraining teams, building redundancy and failsafes — that all took two to three times as long as we planned. We learned that integration is not a project with an end date; it is a process that compounds over years.

We were too optimistic about scale economics in the early years. We believed that putting multiple service companies on one platform would drop overhead by a predictable percentage. The truth was messier. Some functions scaled beautifully; others revealed new costs we had not anticipated. Technology and people infrastructure scaled well. Dispatch and field operations had more friction than we expected. We had to build new thinking around what actually gets cheaper and what just gets bigger.

We moved too slowly on some decisions. We held onto legacy vendors and systems longer than we should have because they were familiar and they worked — even when they were more expensive and harder to manage than modern alternatives. Cost of staying the same is not zero, but it feels like zero until the moment it does not. We have shifted to moving faster on those kinds of decisions.

What we believe now

We believe that durable Canadian businesses matter. The financial world loves stories about scaling fast and selling higher. We are interested in companies that can be run for fifty years, that become part of the fabric of their communities, and that their teams are proud to work for. Those businesses are not made of unicorn metrics; they are made of reliable service, fair pricing, treated employees and compounding reputation.

We believe that the operating holding company model makes sense for a certain class of business — the kind that is capital-efficient, geographically distributable, and benefits from shared infrastructure but not from centralized decision-making on the ground. It is not a model that works everywhere, and we do not pretend it does. But where it fits, it is powerful.

We believe that capital allocation is the parent's real job. Any investor can write checks. The hard part is deciding which businesses deserve more capital, which deserve less, where to place new bets, and what to shut down. That decision-making discipline is what separates a holding company from a financial vehicle. We take that seriously.

We believe in staying Canadian. We have considered expanding into other markets and other countries, and we may do so. But we remain builders first and empire-builders second. We move into a new market only when we have real operating expertise to apply and a reason to believe we can build something durable there. That selectivity limits our size, which is exactly the point.

What comes next

We are now evaluating a small number of Canadian acquisitions. We are exploring selected international markets to understand whether there are opportunities where our expertise transfers and where the conditions are right to build. We are investing in the technology and applied AI that can accelerate and deepen what our operating companies do. We are being deliberate about all of it.

This is what I believe: if you can build a real operating company once, and you have the discipline to keep systems sharp and people engaged, you can do it again. The parent is there to let you. That has been true for nearly twenty years. It will be true for the next twenty, if we stay disciplined about who we are and what we will not do.
